✦ CAREBOT-01 · The Connection
The Jacquard loom (1804) was the first binary input device. Holes in a card meant raise the thread. Solid meant lower it. Charles Babbage saw this and built the punch card into his Analytical Engine. Ada Lovelace wrote programs for that machine — making her the first programmer. Every computer you have ever used runs on logic invented by a weaver.
The textile industry and the computer industry are the same industry, separated by 150 years. Both ran on the labor of women whose names we rarely learn. The Lowell Mill Girls were the first industrial labor force in America. The ENIAC programmers were called "computers" before the machine was. The rope mothers supervised fiber factories using the same binary twist logic — Z-twist / S-twist, over / under, 1 / 0 — that now runs your phone. The thread and the circuit are the same logic. The labor that built both was the same labor. The erasure was the same erasure.
✦ The Thread That Connects
A timeline of the relationship between fiber, labor, and computing — from looms to algorithms.
100,000
BCE
The first use of fiber. Evidence from Neanderthal sites suggests twisted plant cordage — string, sinew, bark fiber — predates modern humans. The ability to twist two strands together to create something stronger than either alone is one of the foundational cognitive leaps in human history. String enables nets, bags, clothing, shelter. Civilization is, in one sense, a species that learned to twist fiber.
36,000
BCE
Flax fibers processed in Dzudzuana Cave, Georgia. Colored and twisted wild flax fibers — some dyed pink, violet, turquoise — found preserved in a cave in the Caucasus. These are not raw fibers. They are processed, selected for color, and twisted into thread. Textile production at this date implies specialized knowledge, division of labor, and aesthetic intention. Someone chose the color. Someone knew the process. That someone is statistically likely to have been a woman.
27,000
BCE
The first woven fibers. Archaeological evidence from the Dzudzuana Cave in Georgia (country) shows twisted plant fibers — the earliest known textile technology. Humans are spinning and interlacing materials before pottery, before agriculture, before writing. The logic of over and under, twist and counter-twist, is one of the oldest technologies our species possesses.
5000
BCE
Weaving looms appear across multiple civilizations simultaneously. In Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, and the Andes — independently, without contact — humans develop the loom. The warp threads are fixed; the weft threads pass over and under. Over, under. Over, under. This is binary logic. Every civilization that weaves discovers the same fundamental computational structure. The loom is not a metaphor for computing. It is computing, performed by hand.
3000
BCE
Textile production is women's work across most of the ancient world. In Mesopotamia, temple records document thousands of female textile workers — weavers, spinners, fullers — paid in rations of grain and oil. In ancient Greece, weaving is the defining female virtue: Penelope weaves and unweaves to hold off her suitors; Athena, goddess of wisdom, is also goddess of weaving. The connection between women, craft knowledge, and intelligent pattern-making is ancient and consistent. The erasure of that connection is more recent.
1200s
The horizontal treadle loom spreads across Europe. Foot pedals (treadles) control the raising and lowering of warp threads, freeing both hands to pass the shuttle. The loom becomes faster, more complex patterns become possible. Guild systems form — and immediately exclude women from the most skilled and highest-paid weaving work. Women continue to spin, to card, to finish. Men control the looms. The technology advances. The gendering of who operates it is imposed, not natural.
1700s
The drawloom enables complex patterned silk weaving — brocades, damasks, tapestries. A second worker, the "draw boy," pulls cords above the loom to raise specific warp threads for each row of the pattern. This is manual programming: someone reads the pattern, selects the correct threads, executes one row, repeats. The draw boy is often a child. The pattern designer is often not credited. The labor of translating a visual pattern into thread-by-thread instructions is the same labor Ada Lovelace will perform for Babbage's machine 100 years later.
1000s–
1500s
The wool trade builds medieval Europe. Wool textiles from England, Flanders, and Florence are the most valuable traded commodity in Europe — the oil of the medieval economy. Entire cities exist to produce, finish, and trade cloth. The Medici bank begins as a wool trading operation. Edward III of England weaponizes wool export as economic warfare. Thread is currency. Textile knowledge is power. The women who spin the raw wool — spinsters — are so central to the economy that their job title becomes the legal term for an unmarried woman.
1500s–
1600s
Cotton arrives in Europe from India and the Americas. The East India Company trades in Indian cotton cloth — calico, muslin, chintz — so successfully that English and French wool merchants petition their governments to ban it. Cotton is lighter, cheaper, washable, and printable. The textile industry fractures between old wool interests and new cotton capital. The battle over fiber is also a battle over empire, labor, and whose knowledge counts.
1733
The flying shuttle doubles weaving speed — and immediately creates a bottleneck. Weavers can now consume thread faster than spinners can produce it. The demand for faster spinning drives the invention of the spinning jenny (1764), the water frame (1769), and the spinning mule (1779). One mechanical innovation triggers a cascade. The Industrial Revolution begins not with steam but with thread — with the problem of keeping up with a faster loom. Every step is powered by women's labor rendered newly mechanical.
1764–
1779
The spinning jenny, water frame, and spinning mule mechanize spinning. James Hargreaves, Richard Arkwright, Samuel Crompton — three men credited with inventions that displaced thousands of female hand-spinners. The domestic spinning wheel, tended by women in their homes for income, is made obsolete in a generation. The word "spinster" — from the women who spun — becomes a term of social failure. The work that built the medieval economy is automated away. The women who performed it are not compensated, retrained, or remembered.
1790s
The first textile factories in America. Samuel Slater smuggles British mill designs to Rhode Island and builds the first American cotton mill in 1793. Child labor — children as young as seven — operates the machinery. The American textile industry is built on the same model as the British: extract maximum labor at minimum cost from those with the least power. Within a generation, the workforce shifts to young women. The factory system and the gendering of textile labor arrive together.
1804
Joseph Marie Jacquard invents the programmable loom. Punched cards control which threads rise and fall — holes = 1, solid = 0. The first binary input device. Pattern complexity is now limited only by the number of cards, not the skill of the weaver. The textile industry is automated. Thousands of weavers lose their livelihoods.
1811
The Luddites. Skilled textile workers in England smash power looms and stocking frames. They are not anti-technology — they are protesting the use of machinery to destroy craft labor and wage protections. They are framed as technophobes. The framing holds to this day. Their actual argument — that technology should serve workers, not replace them — has never been answered.
1820s
The Lowell Mill Girls. Young women from New England farms come to work in the cotton mills. They are the first industrial labor force in America — mostly between 15 and 35, literate, organized. They run literary magazines. They organize some of the first American labor strikes when wages are cut. Their labor builds the American textile industry. Their names are largely forgotten.
1820s–
1860s
The rope mothers (Seilmutter). Female supervisors of ropewalk factories — the long, narrow buildings where rope was twisted from raw fiber. Rope-making uses a binary alternation: Z-twist and S-twist, two directions of rotation applied in sequence to create strength. Over, under. Over, under. 1, 0. The same binary logic as weaving, the same binary logic as computing. These women managed teams, controlled tension, and ensured quality in highly technical industrial operations. Their job title contained the word "mother." Their work built the rope that held ships, bridges, and mines together. It does not appear in computing history.
1837
Charles Babbage designs the Analytical Engine — the first programmable mechanical computer. He adapts Jacquard's punch card system as the input mechanism. The loom and the computer are now directly connected by the same physical object: a card with holes.
1843
Ada Lovelace writes the first algorithm. Daughter of Lord Byron and mathematician Anne Isabella Milbanke — who studied math specifically to counteract Byron's "poetic madness." Ada translates a paper on Babbage's engine and adds notes three times the length of the original, including what is now recognized as the first computer program. She is 27. Her work is not fully recognized until the 1950s.
1890
Herman Hollerith adapts Jacquard cards for the US Census tabulating machine. The punch card moves from textile mill to data processing. Hollerith's company eventually becomes IBM. The 80-column IBM punch card — used until the 1970s — is the direct descendant of Jacquard's loom card. Every COBOL programmer punched a card that a weaver designed.
1945
Six women program ENIAC — the first general-purpose electronic computer. Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, Ruth Lichterman. They are called "computers" — the word for a person who computes — before the machine takes the name. They receive no credit at the public unveiling. Their contributions are classified for decades.
Now
Women make up less than 30% of the AI workforce. The same logic that erased the mill girls, the rope mothers, Ada Lovelace, and the ENIAC women continues to operate. The domestic space — the original R&D lab — remains uncredited. The thread that connects all of this is not a metaphor. It is a punch card. It is a loom. It is a circuit. It is the same binary logic, running continuously since 1804.
✦ Object 01
The Jacquard Loom
A mechanical loom programmed by punched cards. Holes control which threads rise. Solid controls which fall. This is binary code. This is the ancestor of every computer ever built. The textile industry invented programmable automation in 1804. It ran on the labor of women. It has been credited to engineers ever since.

✦ Object 02
The Punch Card
Herman Hollerith adapts Jacquard's cards for the 1890 Census. IBM standardizes 80 columns. Every programmer from 1890 to 1980 submits their work on cards descended from a weaver's pattern cards. The computer and the loom share the same input device. One is called technology. The other is called craft. The distinction is gendered.

✦ Object 03
The Weave Pattern
Plain weave: over one thread, under the next. Over, under. Over, under. 1, 0. 1, 0. Weaving is binary computation performed by hand. The Jacquard loom automates this. Binary code names the same logic. The weave pattern is the original algorithm. The person who designed it was not called a programmer.
Image: images/threads03.png — Plain weave diagram showing over/under binary logic
✦ Object 04
The Rope
Rope-making mills used Z-twist and S-twist — two directions of fiber rotation, alternated for strength. Binary logic. Rope mothers (Seilmutter) supervised these operations in European mills — female overseers of highly technical fiber work. The CAREtech project includes rope mothers in the EMBRACE Effect framework: women whose technical labor was named after a domestic object and then forgotten.
Image: images/threads04.png — Ropewalk factory interior
✦ Object 05
The Mill Spindle
The Lowell Mill Girls tended spindles — machines that twisted raw cotton into thread at high speed, in buildings with no ventilation, in 14-hour shifts. They were the first industrial workforce in America. They organized strikes. They ran literary magazines. They are remembered, when remembered at all, as simple farm girls who briefly worked in factories before marriage. They were the foundation of the American industrial economy.
Image: images/threads05.png — Lowell mill interior, 1840s
✦ Object 06
The ENIAC Panel
The ENIAC women — six of them — hand-wired a room-sized computer by consulting logic diagrams and physically connecting patch cables. They were human computers before the machine took the name. At the public unveiling, they were not introduced. The generals and engineers were. The women who wrote the first programs are in photographs from that day. For decades, no one knew who they were.
