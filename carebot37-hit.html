✦ CAREBOT-37 · The Irony
Pink used to be a boy color. It was considered strong, bold, a derivative of red. In 1918, stores recommended pink for boys, blue for girls. By 1940, marketing flipped it. Now pink means domesticity, femininity, caregiving — and the objects of that labor: the ironing board, the laundry basket, the vacuum, the desk. The color that once meant strength now marks the work nobody pays for.
The flip happened in one generation, driven by marketing, not biology. The same industry that assigned pink to women then charged them 17% more for pink products. The color brands domestic labor — the labor that holds up the economy while being excluded from it. You can have it in pink. The iron. The vacuum. The watering can. The laundry basket. The desk. The electronics. The wig. You can have your labor in any color, as long as it stays yours.
✦ A Short History of Pink
The gender-color assignment we treat as natural is less than 100 years old — and it was manufactured.
1700s
Pink is a masculine color. Considered a derivative of red — strong, decided, passionate. Blue is the feminine color — delicate, dainty, associated with the Virgin Mary. Boys wear pink. Girls wear blue. This is not controversial.
1880s
Before widely available dyes, all young children wore white. Boys and girls both wore long white dresses. Both wore long hair. Gender was not encoded in clothing for small children. Children were dressed for practicality — white cotton can be bleached.
1918
Earnshaw's Infants' Department trade publication states: "The generally accepted rule is pink for the boys, and blue for the girls. The reason is that pink, being a more decided and stronger color, is more suitable for the boy, while blue, which is more delicate and dainty, is prettier for the girl." This is the industry standard. Pink = boys.
1927
Time Magazine publishes a chart showing sex-appropriate colors according to leading US department stores: Filene's in Boston, Best & Co. in New York City, Halle's in Cleveland, Marshall Field in Chicago — all recommending pink for boys.
1940s
Marketing flips it. Baby boom. Mass consumer market. Targeted advertising to mothers. Pink becomes the girls' color; blue becomes the boys'. The switch is driven by manufacturers, not nature. One generation. One industry decision. Permanent.
1960s–70s
Women's liberation movement pushes back. Unisex baby clothing becomes fashionable. The pink-for-girls coding dips. Parents resist the binary. Neutral clothing returns.
1980s
Prenatal sex testing becomes widely available. Parents can find out before birth. Retail responds: two coded nurseries, two coded wardrobes, two coded toy aisles. Pink-for-girls entrenches permanently in American consumer culture. The toy aisle splits.
Now
Pink marks domestic labor objects sold to women: ironing boards, vacuums, laundry baskets, watering cans, desks, electronics, wigs. The color that once encoded strength now encodes caregiving. And those pink products cost 17% more. The marketing that built the association also profits from it.
✦ Object 01
The Pink Ironing Board
Ironing is domestic labor — unpaid, invisible, gendered. Pink the ironing board and it becomes a product, a purchase, a lifestyle object. The labor it represents — hours of standing, steaming, pressing — is not compensated. The board is sold at a premium for the pink version. The work it enables is valued at zero. The board is cute. The labor is free.
Pink was once the color of strength. Now it marks the object of the labor that holds up the economy while being excluded from wage calculation.
✦ Object 02
The Pink Laundry Basket
Laundry is the most frequently recurring domestic task. It never ends — wash, dry, fold, repeat. Pink the basket and it signals: this is hers. This is a woman's object in a woman's space doing a woman's work. The basket costs more in pink. The time to fill and empty it — hours each week, thousands of hours over a lifetime — does not appear in any national economic measurement.
The CAREtech laundry basket head: checkmark eye for skills acknowledged, X eye for labor discarded. The basket holds what isn't counted.
✦ Object 03
The Pink Vacuum
Vacuum cleaners were initially marketed to men as industrial efficiency machines — technology, engineering, power. As the product moved into the home and became associated with women's housework, it became pink, pastel, "elegant." The technology didn't change. The gender coding changed. And the price went up for the pink version. The same machine. Different color. Different customer. Higher cost.
Gendered products are 17% more expensive than male gendered counterparts (Georgetown Law Journal, 2025). The vacuum is a case study.
✦ Object 04
The Pink Watering Can
Gardening was historically a male domain — land management, agriculture, cultivation. As kitchen gardens became women's domestic responsibility and gardening became "hobby" rather than "labor," the objects pinkified. A pink watering can signals leisure, femininity, decorativeness. But the labor of tending growing things — food, home, children — is the same labor. The color reclassifies it. From production to decoration. From work to lifestyle.
✦ Object 05
The Pink Desk
A desk is a site of intellectual work, productivity, output. A pink desk signals: this is a girl's workspace — cute, soft, decorative. A girl's work. Not serious work. Not paid work. The same desk in black or white sells to executives. In pink it sells to teenage girls and mothers who work from home. The color assignment changes the perceived value of the labor performed at it. The desk is the same. The cultural weight of the work done there is not.
The rolling pink desk in CARETECH_CEO.exe moves through the performance space as the CEO layer — authority, mobility, control — operated by a body in an apron.
✦ Object 06
The Pink Electronics
Pink laptops, pink phones, pink earbuds. Technology is a male-coded field — less than 30% of the AI workforce is female (Global Gender Gap Report 2023). Pink electronics mark women as consumers of technology, not creators of it. The pink version of the same device costs more. The women who design the algorithms that run inside them are underrepresented, underpaid, and their work is credited to the product, not to them.
Women make up less than 30% of the AI workforce and it drops to 15% at senior levels. Pink electronics signal welcome without power.
✦ Object 07
The Pink Wig
Pink wigs are costume, performance, femininity made visible and exaggerated. In CARETECH_CEO.exe the pink wig passes between objects — placed on the chassis, the vacuum, the projector, the ironing board. Each object it lands on becomes gendered. The wig reveals the mechanism: femininity is not inside the object. It is assigned by color, by accessory, by marketing. Remove the wig. The object is neutral again. The gender was always a costume.
✦ Object 08
The Pink Tablecloth
Setting a table is hospitality labor — invisible, recurring, socially mandatory. A pink tablecloth marks the table as feminine domestic space. It was set by someone. It will be cleared by someone. That someone is statistically likely to be a woman — mothers perform 73% of cognitive household labor. The tablecloth signals welcome, warmth, care. It does not signal who cleaned it, pressed it, chose it, stored it, and will do it again next week.
✦ The Original Pink — Before the Flip
In 1918, pink was the strong color. Blue was the delicate one.
"The generally accepted rule is pink for the boys, and blue for the girls. The reason is that pink, being a more decided and stronger color, is more suitable for the boy, while blue, which is more delicate and dainty, is prettier for the girl." — Earnshaw's Infants' Department, June 1918

The same color. A different century. A completely different meaning. The meaning was never in the color. It was always in the marketing. The strength was reassigned. The labor was kept.
✦ The Pink Tax — The Color Costs More
Pink products are more expensive. The color that marks domestic labor also marks price markup.
Women are charged more for products that are functionally identical to men's versions — differentiated only by color, packaging, and scent. The same razor. The same shampoo. The same pen. The same desk. Pink = premium. The color that encodes domesticity also extracts payment for the privilege of having it.
