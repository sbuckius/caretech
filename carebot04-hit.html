✦ Research Notes

How much does luck matter in AI hiring?

A lot — and it's not just a feeling. ZipRecruiter's CEO has estimated that the large majority of submitted resumes are read by software before any human sees them, with only a small fraction making it to a person.[1] Harvard Business School's "Hidden Workers" research found that 88% of employers surveyed admitted their own applicant-tracking systems regularly filter out qualified candidates — often because a resume used different wording than the job posting, was saved in the wrong file format, or used a layout the parser couldn't read correctly.[2] None of that has anything to do with whether someone can do the job.

And even when a human does the evaluating, it turns out to be close to a coin flip. Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman and his co-authors found that if all you know is that one candidate seemed better than another in an interview, there's only about a 56–61% chance that candidate is actually the better one — barely above chance.[3] They call this "noise": different interviewers reaching wildly inconsistent conclusions from the exact same information, with no consistent direction to the error the way bias has. So yes — luck, in the form of who happens to interview you, what mood they're in, and which arbitrary details catch their eye, is a real and measured part of how hiring decisions get made.

How AI hiring tools have shown bias

Bias shows up well before "AI" enters the picture, and AI tools have repeatedly inherited it. The landmark Bertrand & Mullainathan field experiment sent nearly 5,000 fictitious resumes to real job ads and found that resumes with white-sounding names received 50% more callbacks than identical resumes with Black-sounding names.[4] A 2024 follow-up study, which filed 83,000 applications to Fortune 500 employers, found that gap is still showing up today, with some companies calling back white-sounding names roughly 9–24% more often.[5]

AI systems can pick up and automate exactly this kind of pattern. Amazon built an experimental AI resume-screening tool between 2014–2017 that taught itself, from a decade of mostly male historical hires, to downgrade resumes containing the word "women's" and favor male-coded language — Amazon ultimately scrapped the project.[6] More recent research has also documented disability bias in GPT-based resume screening, and bias against names and characteristics signaling race, gender, and other traits across other AI hiring and screening tools.[7]

✦ The irony

Put this all together and the rock-paper-scissors email above starts to look less like a quirky misstep and more like an honest accident: a game built on pure chance, sent to someone whose actual chances are already being shaped by chance — algorithmic noise[3] and, in some pipelines, outright bias[4][5][6][7]. When the stakes are someone's rent or health insurance, wrapping that reality in a "fun engagement activity" doesn't lighten the moment — it trivializes it. Gamifying a high-stakes, luck-and-bias-laden process reads as patronizing, not playful: it asks a job seeker to smile through a process they have little control over, instead of being honest about how arbitrary and unequal that process can be.
