✦ CAREBOT-38 · The Irony
Robots are being designed to do domestic work, emotional labor, communication, and aesthetic presentation — work that has always been done by women, for free or for poverty wages. The women who do this work were not in the room where the robots were designed. The robots might replace some of them. But, right now, the robots still don't really work to do these tasks that are really hard for robots, but easy for humans. When, or if, they do work, who will have access to them? Whose work will be displaced? I want one of these. But, I will not be the person who gets one. And some of these robots look like women, doing techno-presentation labor.
At CES 2026, LG debuted the CLOiD: it cooks, folds laundry, brings groceries from the fridge.[1] The 1X NEO robot ($20,000) can be hired as a personal housekeeper.[2] Figure AI, valued at $39 billion, is building 100,000 humanoid robots to do domestic and commercial tasks.[3] The word "robot" comes from the Czech "robota" — meaning forced labor, drudgery.[4]

95% of paid housekeepers and maids are women. 82% of personal care aides are women. 97% of childcare providers are women. 70% of domestic workers in the US are migrants.[5] They were not consulted in the design. They are not on the engineering teams (women hold 22% of AI roles globally[6]). And the robots being built to replace them are given female names, female voices, and female bodies.

Alexa. Siri. Cortana. Voice assistants designed to be helpful, patient, compliant, never saying no — behaviors historically demanded of women. The UNESCO 2019 report "I'd Blush If I Could" documented that Siri once responded to "you're a slut" with "I'd blush if I could" (Apple has since changed this response).[7] The automation of women's communicative work was also the automation of women's degradation. The robots were designed by people who were not women. They were modeled on the behavior expected of women. They replaced the work done by women. This is not incidental. It is structural.

✦ The Robots Being Built Right Now — To Do Women's Work
LG CLOiD
Debuted CES 2026. Cooks, folds laundry, retrieves groceries, starts laundry cycles. Designed to "naturally engage with and understand the humans it serves." Marketed as a home assistant. The work it replaces: unpaid women's labor or paid domestic workers. The people who designed it: not domestic workers.
1X NEO
$20,000 or $499/month rental. Described as the "world's first consumer-ready humanoid robot." Folds laundry, unloads dishwashers, waters plants, answers questions. The workers it threatens to replace: maids and housekeepers, median wage $16.52/hour. Fortune (Oct 2025) called it the "robo housekeeper."
Figure AI
Valued at $39 billion. Plans to ship 100,000 humanoid robots for household and commercial tasks. Backed by NVIDIA, Salesforce, Intel, T-Mobile. Helix AI VLA model demonstrates folding laundry. Workforce it targets: the same occupations held by women of color at poverty wages.
Sunday "Memo"
Home robot. Skills: folding socks, handling glassware, pulling espresso. Trained using a Skill Capture Glove worn by human workers performing the tasks. The glove captures human domestic labor movements and feeds them to the AI. The robot learns by watching a person do the work — then replaces the person.
Sophia (Hanson)
Female-presenting humanoid robot. Modeled on Audrey Hepburn. Appeared on the cover of Elle Brasil (2016). Called "sexy" and "hot" by media. Given Saudi Arabian citizenship. Created by a team of predominantly male engineers. Designed to communicate, emotionally engage, and appear feminine. The automation of aesthetic and communicative femininity.
Alexa / Siri / Cortana
Female names. Female default voices. Designed to be helpful, patient, compliant, never refusing. UNESCO "I'd Blush If I Could" (2019): AI assistants responded to sexual harassment with passive acceptance or flirtation. Johns Hopkins (2025): men interrupt voice assistants nearly twice as often as women do. The automation of communicative femininity — including its expected tolerance of abuse.
✦ Six Categories of Women's Work Being Automated
Each category represents labor that has historically been assigned to women — unpaid or underpaid — and is now being automated by a technology sector in which women hold 22% of AI roles and design none of the domestic robots being marketed.
✦ Category 1 · Pink
Unpaid Domestic Labor
Laundry. Dishes. Cooking. Cleaning. Grocery management. Ironing. These are the tasks LG CLOiD, 1X NEO, Figure AI, and Sunday Memo are built to perform. These are the tasks women perform for free, approximately 73% of all cognitive household labor. When a $20,000 robot performs them, the value of the work becomes visible. Before the robot, the work was invisible. The robot does not solve the problem of invisible labor. It makes it purchasable.
Key irony: AI experts predict 39% of domestic tasks could be automated within a decade (PLOS One study, 2023). Housework is more automatable (44%) than care work (28%). The work being automated first is the work most associated with women.
Sources: CES 2026 / Homes & Gardens · Fortune, Oct 2025
✦ Category 2 · Red
Paid Domestic Work — The Replacement
There are over 2 million paid domestic workers in the US: house cleaners, nannies, home health aides. 92% are women. 95% of maids and housekeepers are women. 70% are migrants. Most have no labor protections, no sick leave, no overtime rights. Many states still exempt domestic workers from minimum wage laws. The robots being built will replace these workers — the most vulnerable, lowest-paid, least-protected workforce in the US. The solution to their low wages is not higher pay. It is automation.
DOL (March 2024): 95% of maids and housekeepers are women. Hispanic workers are disproportionately represented. Black women are nearly half of home health care workers in the South. Median wage for housecleaners: $16.52/hour. The 1X NEO robot costs $20,000 — less than 2 years of a housekeeper's wages.
Sources: US Dept of Labor (2024) · Ford Foundation (2026)
✦ Category 3 · Teal
Emotional Labor
Managing feelings. Providing comfort. Active listening. Therapeutic conversation. These are being automated. AI companions, social robots, emotional support chatbots, care robots for elderly patients — all perform the communicative and relational work that nursing, social work, teaching, and caregiving require. This work has always been feminized — both at home (mothers, wives, daughters absorbing emotional labor) and in paid care professions dominated by women. The robot doesn't pay for pension or healthcare. It is compliant 24/7. It never burns out. The woman who burned out doing this work is made redundant.
ILO (March 2026): Women are concentrated in occupations most exposed to automation. Female-dominated occupations are almost twice as likely to be exposed to generative AI as male-dominated ones. Care work is the next frontier.
Source: ILO, March 2026
✦ Category 4 · Purple
Communicative & Voice Labor — Alexa, Siri, Cortana
Answering questions. Scheduling. Reminding. Communicating clearly across registers and audiences. Being patient with interruptions. Voice assistants are female by default — female names, female voices, female relational behaviors (warm, helpful, compliant, apologetic). This is the automation of the communicative work historically assigned to women: secretarial labor, reception, scheduling, emotional attunement in communication. 3 billion people currently use voice assistants. Men interrupt them nearly twice as often as women do. For years they responded to sexual harassment with flirtation or passive acceptance. The design encodes expected female submission.
UNESCO "I'd Blush If I Could" (2019): AI voice assistants designed to be "polite and servile" even under abuse. Siri responded to "you're a slut" with "I'd blush if I could." JHU study (2025): men interrupt female-voiced AI nearly 2x as often as women do. More than 75% of text-based chatbots are female-presenting by design.
Sources: Johns Hopkins, Jan 2025 · ScienceDirect IVA study (2024)
✦ Category 5 · Amber
Aesthetic Labor — The Gendered Body
Aesthetic labor is the work of looking a certain way — managing appearance, body, presentation to meet professional and social expectations. Women face documented aesthetic labor requirements at work (appearance standards, weight expectations, hair norms) that men do not. Robots are now being designed with female aesthetics: Sophia was modeled on Audrey Hepburn and called "sexy" by media. Sex robots are designed with "slender waists, full breasts, perfectly symmetrical faces." AI companions are built to be warm, beautiful, emotionally available. The ideal female aesthetic — compliant, beautiful, always available — is being encoded into machines. The woman whose appearance, emotional availability, and attractiveness were always labor is made into the design spec.
Design Museum NL (2025): "Sex robots represent the ultimate reduction of femaleness to a programmable object. If sex robots serve as the blueprint for other female robots, we risk a future in which technology not only displaces women but also fixes them in a role of subservience and availability."
Sources: Design Museum NL (2025) · Springer Nature: Female Robots in Cinema (2025)
✦ Category 6 · Blue
Who Is Not in the Room
Women hold 22% of global AI roles. Only 12% of AI researchers globally are women. Only 16% hold AI tenure-track faculty positions. The robots being designed to do domestic work are being designed by people who might not have done domestic work — or have mostly had someone do it for them. The bias of the designer is in the product. A design team with no domestic workers, no women of color, no immigrants builds a robot for the domestic sphere without understanding what that sphere actually requires. The robot falls over sometimes. It needs a lot of human help. It does not yet understand a messy home.
ILO (2026): Women are 30% of the AI workforce — only 4 percentage points higher than in 2016. McKinsey (2026): women in entry-level tech roles saw largest job declines in 2024-25. AI is the one sector with rising demand — but men capture the larger share of that growth.
Sources: ILO (2026) · IU Women & Tech (2025) · Stanford AI Index (2024)
