✦ CAREBOT-36 · The Irony
College graduates can't find entry-level jobs. The advice: just become a CEO. The reality: the CEO chair has been occupied by almost exclusively one demographic for decades — and specific, documented barriers keep it that way.
30% of 2025 graduates secured jobs in their field. 33% are unemployed. The advice given by Indeed, GCU, Crummer, Crimson Education, and Inc. Magazine: pivot to entrepreneurship, become a CEO.

~85% of Fortune 500 CEOs are white men. Women hold 11%, an all-time high. Only two Black women have ever run a Fortune 500 company, and none has ever led a top-10 tech company. Black founders received 0.48% of US venture capital in 2024. The advice does not mention the broken rung, the glass cliff, the sponsorship gap, the double bind, or the 4.8-year tenure penalty. It addresses the individual. It ignores the structure.

✦ The Specific Barriers — What the Advice Doesn't Say
The "just become a CEO" advice skips six documented, named, researched structural mechanisms that make that path significantly harder depending on who you are. These are not excuses. They are data.
✦ Barrier 1 · Women + People of Color
The Broken Rung
The biggest barrier to the CEO chair doesn't happen at the top — it happens at the first step up to manager. For every 100 men promoted to their first manager role, only 93 women make the same leap, and just 74 women of color do. Women are 49% of entry-level employees and 29% of the C-suite. The path loses women at every rung — not because of ambition, but because of who gets promoted.
McKinsey / LeanIn Women in the Workplace (2025): Women underrepresented at every pipeline level for the 11th consecutive year. Only 29% of C-suite roles. Women of color: just 7% of C-suite.
Source: McKinsey / LeanIn, 2025
✦ Barrier 2 · Women
The Sponsorship Gap
Mentors advise. Sponsors open doors. Women have fewer sponsors — the senior leaders who put your name forward for promotion, plum assignments, and stretch opportunities. Only 31% of entry-level women have a sponsor vs 45% of men at the same level. Without a sponsor, you do the work. Someone else gets promoted. The advice to network and connect never mentions that the networking itself is gendered.
INSEAD (Feb 2026): "Women are under-sponsored. While mentors advise, sponsors open doors." The barrier is not a shortage of capable women, but how boards define readiness and who gets advocated for.
Source: INSEAD Knowledge, Feb 2026; Keystone Partners, Feb 2026
✦ Barrier 3 · People of Color
The Glass Cliff
After the murder of George Floyd in 2020, many companies hired Black women into leadership roles — often at companies that were already in crisis. This is the glass cliff: women and people of color are more likely to be appointed to leadership during organizational failure, not stability. They inherit problems they didn't create. When the company continues to struggle, the failure gets attributed to them personally — reinforcing stereotypes about their fitness to lead.
Center for Talent Innovation (2023): Black executives are 2.5x more likely to be prematurely removed from leadership roles under "organizational restructuring." Female leaders are 24% more likely to be fired than male counterparts.
Source: Catalyst, 2024; HBR, Mar 2024
✦ Barrier 4 · Women + People of Color
The Double Bind
Be assertive: you're "aggressive." Be collaborative: you're "not leadership material." Behaviors rewarded as leadership potential in white men are penalized in women and people of color. The acceptable behavioral range is narrower. As one executive of color described it: "I'm either too direct or not assertive enough." The standard shifts depending on who you are. The advice to "develop leadership skills" doesn't name this.
Perceptual imprinting research (2024): People's visual templates for how leaders look have been skewed by constant exposure to white male leaders — making women and people of color appear "less like leaders" regardless of their actual qualifications.
Source: PMC / Leadership Perception Study, 2024; Bridgespan
✦ Barrier 5 · People of Color
The Capital Access Gap
The advice to "start your own company and become a CEO" requires startup capital. In 2024, Black founders received 0.48% of all US venture capital funding despite Black Americans being 13.4% of the population. Women founders received approximately 2%. VC networks operate through existing connections — connections that have been historically built between white male investors and white male founders. The advice skips the capital requirement entirely.
UN Global Compact (2025): "Women entrepreneurs still receive less than 2% of global venture capital funding." The care economy is undervalued. Women-led sectors are chronically underinvested.
Source: UN Global Compact, 2025; Crunchbase 2024 Diversity in VC
✦ Barrier 6 · Women
The Domestic Labor Tax
Women with partners are more than 3x as likely as men to be responsible for all or most housework. Nearly 25% of women who express reluctance about promotion cite personal obligations — vs 15% of men. Remote work, which helps women manage caregiving, simultaneously reduces their visibility and access to sponsors. The flexibility-visibility tradeoff is a documented mechanism. The advice to "network aggressively and seek promotion" ignores who is doing the second shift at home.
McKinsey / LeanIn (2026): Entry-level women working remotely are 1.5x less likely to be promoted than in-office counterparts. The flexibility that manages caregiving reduces visibility that produces sponsorship.
Source: Keystone / McKinsey Women in the Workplace, Feb 2026
