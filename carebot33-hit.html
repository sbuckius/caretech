CEO · Robot · Human

A performance in a 4 × 4 ft square full of domestic objects. A robot that moves like a robot. A camera strapped to the robot's head. Instructions written by AI. A CEO who acts like a CEO who cares. And a human — a mother — who cares, who has the experiences, and who really wants to see the system change.

The Three Performers
Performer 01
The CEO
acts like a CEO who cares.
The CEO believes everything on this website — the EMBRACE Effect, the pillars, all of it. The CEO speaks the language of care fluently and means it. The CEO acts like the caring, equitable technological future might actually be possible.

The CEO is the person the human wishes existed. In this performance, for one hour, the CEO does exist — and the audience gets to feel what that would be like.

And yet: the CEO is stuck in the current technology system in many ways. The CEO uses AI — to test its limits, and to get work done. The CEO feels conflicted about this, and has more questions than answers. The question the CEO carries through the whole performance: how can it be used ethically in the current technology climate?
Performer 02
The Robot
tries. fails. keeps going.
The robot moves like a robot — stiff, stepped, deliberate. A camera is strapped to its head, recording everything from the machine's point of view. It moves domestic objects around a 4 × 4 ft square the way humanoid robots do, and it has trouble navigating even that.

It tries to follow all of the directions the bots (AI) generated. It fails. When it can't keep up, it just does its own thing. Nothing is completed. And still — the robot is the one who pays the workers.
Performer 03
The Human
cares. has the experiences.
The human believes everything on this website and wishes it were all possible. The human wishes she could pay the workers more. The human wishes the CEO were real.

The human is a mother — and she knows that caring for people and caring for technology both require boundary-setting, evaluation, and embodied experience. The human is the person at the end who really wants to see the system change.

What Happens, In Order
1
The square. All of the domestic objects are placed inside a 4 × 4 ft square. The robot must work inside it. The robot has trouble navigating it — the space that a mother crosses ten thousand times a day without thinking is, for the machine, nearly impassable.
2
The instructions. The directions were created by AI — by the bots. The robot tries to follow all of them. Every instruction is confident, optimized, and fluent. None of them account for the actual room, the actual objects, the actual body.
3
The failure. The robot cannot keep up. It picks things up and puts them down somewhere else. It bumps the basket. It abandons the kettle mid-task. Sometimes, when the instructions come too fast, it just does its own thing. Nothing is actually completed with the domestic objects — and the domestic work, the real work, remains undone, the way it always does when it is handed to a system that doesn't understand it.
4
The camera. A camera is strapped to the robot's head, recording what is seen from the perspective of the robot's head. The machine sees the objects. It sees the tasks. It does not see the care. The recording is the evidence.
5
The payment. The robot pays the workers. The human watches, and wishes she could pay them more. The machine disburses; the human values. The amount is never enough, and the human is the only one in the room who feels that.
6
The CEO. The CEO acts like a CEO who cares — and the CEO does believe it, all of it, every pillar of the EMBRACE Effect. The CEO acts like it might be possible. For the length of the performance, the caring CEO exists. But the CEO is stuck in the current technology system in many ways: the CEO uses AI to test its limits and to get work done, feels conflicted about doing so, and holds more questions than answers. The CEO's open question hangs over the square: how can it be used ethically in the current technology climate?
7
The human. The human is the person at the end. The human cares. The human has the experiences. The human believes everything on this website and wishes it were all possible. The human is a mother who knows that caring — for people and for technology — requires boundary-setting, evaluation, and embodied experience. The human really wants to see change in the system. The performance ends with her.
What the Mother Knows
Caring for people and caring for technology require the same things. She has done both. That is the expertise no instruction set contains.
Boundary-Setting
Care is not saying yes to everything. A mother knows when to say no, when to say not yet, and when to say not like this. Technology needs the same boundaries — and someone with the authority to set them.
Evaluation
Care is constant assessment: is this working, is this safe, is this good for the person in front of me? A mother evaluates all day, every day, without a dashboard. Technology must be evaluated the same way — continuously, contextually, by someone who is paying attention.
Embodied Experience
The knowledge lives in the hands, in the back, in the 3 a.m. judgment call. It cannot be scraped, prompted, or strapped to a head. The robot recorded everything and understood none of it. The human understood everything and was recorded by no one.

CAREtech · A Note on AI
A Note on AI (Automation)

Sarah used AI (automation) to help construct the HTML to set up this website by prompting it with details about the idea, the features, the parts, and the framework. Sarah believes that many things should not be automated using AI and that users should be cautious about the inaccuracies and bias in the system, exploitation of workers, the environmental impact, and the unethical use of the work of people without compensation, consent, or attribution.

Another issue that might negatively impact humans is the process by which it is forced into our lives and technology and work — and thus could result in our becoming reliant on it, without the ability to back out. Once everyone must use it, it forces the speed and style and aesthetics into the system, which requires everyone to utilize it to stay relevant and up to speed. This is a concern, especially because the design, deployment, and use is in the power of a few individuals (without guardrails or oversight). Sarah worries about this reliance on the technology when those in power make the cost to use it high — and this creates an equity issue.

While Sarah still struggles with the issues related to how AI (automation) could impact humans and the world, Sarah is experimenting with using it to support education and equity in instances where it could support students and learning. The use of it in this case is debatable, because I used it to help me generate the code for the website that I envisioned. Even though it is close to what I had in my mind, it is very highly possible that merely using it created the images in my mind. This process might result in more and more technology looking more dull and the same. Perhaps this website will end up being a relic of this strange time — a speculative capsule stuck in a weird, slopification automation void.
