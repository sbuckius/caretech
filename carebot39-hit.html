✦ CAREBOT-39 · The Irony
Computing and engineering use the word "mother" to mean the most essential, generative, originating component in any system. The sector that encodes "mother" as its highest technical authority is the sector that has most aggressively excluded mothers from its workforce.
Motherboard. Mother forms. Mother machine. Mother disc. Mother ship. Rope Mother. Mother of All Demos.

In every case: mother = origin, source, generative authority, the controlling center from which everything derives. First attested use of "motherboard" in Electronics magazine, 1965.[1] The first true PC motherboard — the Planar Breadboard — designed by IBM engineer Patty McHugh, 1981.[2] The "Rope Mother" who oversaw the software that landed on the moon: Margaret Hamilton, who coined the term "software engineering."[3] The women who wove the core ropes she designed were called "little old ladies." They had previously worked at textile mills.[3]

The numbers: Women hold roughly 22% of the global AI workforce. Only about 12% of AI researchers worldwide are women.[4] An estimated 455,000 women left the US labor force between January and August 2025.[5] The US has no federal paid family leave program.[6] The word is everywhere in computing. The mothers are being pushed out.

✦ The Full Taxonomy — "Mother" in Computing and Engineering
Across hardware, software, manufacturing, and systems: "mother" usually means the origin, the source, the thing everything else depends on. We removed several terms ("mother chip," "mother clock," "mother copy," "mother node/server," "mother jig/gauge/mold," "mother alloy," "mother pipe/vessel") that we could not find genuine references for — they may exist as someone's shop-floor shorthand, but we couldn't verify them as documented terms. What's left below is either well-documented (motherboard, mother disc, Rope Mother, mother ship) or clearly labeled as a correction.
Hardware · 1965
Motherboard
// main printed circuit board; connects CPU, RAM, storage, all peripherals
First attested in Electronics magazine, 1965 — "the mother of all boards," from which all other components depend. The first true motherboard (Planar Breadboard, 1981) was designed by IBM engineer Patty McHugh. Her board became the standard for every PC since. (Note: earlier versions of Wikipedia's "Motherboard" article didn't name her — the article now credits her by name, alongside Britannica.)
Source: Wikipedia Motherboard · Britannica · Etymonline
Software / Aerospace · 1960s
Rope Mother(s)
// the job title for whoever supervised changes to Apollo's core rope memory software
At MIT's Instrumentation Lab and Raytheon, "rope mother" was an actual job title — the person responsible for overseeing and signing off on integration of changes to the Apollo Guidance Computer's core rope memory before it was physically woven into hardware. There were multiple rope mothers across the program; Margaret Hamilton, profiled below, is the one history remembers. One genuine wrinkle: according to the Computer History Museum's David Brock, the title wasn't strictly gendered in practice — some rope mothers were men. The women who did the actual hand-weaving were separately (and dismissively) nicknamed "LOLs," or "Little Old Ladies."
Source: IEEE Spectrum, "Software as Hardware: Apollo's Rope Memory" · Science News, "Core memory weavers and Navajo women made the Apollo missions possible" · Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum
Software / Unix · 1969+
Parent Process (not "mother process")
// the process that spawns child processes in Unix/Linux
Correction: the standard computing term here is parent process, not "mother process" — an earlier version of this carebot used the wrong term. In Unix/Linux (and every OS since), processes run in a parent-child hierarchy: the parent process creates child processes, which inherit its properties and permissions. Kill the parent and, by default, its children are orphaned. The hierarchy itself is gendered as "parent," not specifically "mother" — worth noting, since most of this carebot's other examples really do use "mother."
Source: Wikipedia, "Parent process"
Machining / Manufacturing
Mother Forms & Mother Machine
// the jigs, dies, and fixtures from which mass production is built; the machine that makes other machines
In tool & die making, the foundational jigs, dies, and fixtures used to anchor mass production are sometimes called "mother forms" — the physical templates from which large-scale, consistent production becomes possible. Related: a "mother machine" is the equipment that makes other machines, treated in some manufacturing writing as the foundation of a nation's industrial capacity. (We could not verify "mother jig," "mother gauge," or "mother mold" as individually attested terms — those have been removed.)
Source: JEELIX, "The History & Evolution of Tool & Die Making" and JEELIX, "Tool and Die Maker: A Comprehensive Guide".
Vinyl / Optical Media
Mother Disc
// intermediate stamper from the master recording used to press all copies
Correction: in vinyl pressing, the lacquer master is electroplated to make a "father" disc (ridges, used as a stamper), which is electroplated again to make the mother disc (grooves, a positive copy of the original). Stampers for actual pressing are then made from the mother — the mother itself is usually archived, not destroyed, so more stampers can be made later. From the mother disc, all that record's stampers — and therefore all its copies — descend.
Source: Yamaha, "How a Vinyl Record Is Made" · Atlas Records
Maritime / Aerospace
Mother Ship
// a large craft that leads, services, or carries smaller craft — in dictionaries since the late 1880s
Unlike most of the trade jargon on this page, mother ship (or "mothership") is a genuinely documented dictionary word: a large ship, aircraft, or spacecraft that carries, leads, or services smaller craft, first recorded around 1885–90. It's used for everything from Apollo's command module (the "mother ship" the Lunar Module returned to) to, by extension, the main office a company's satellite branches answer to.
Source: Merriam-Webster, "Mother Ship" · Wikipedia, "Mother ship" · Collins English Dictionary
✦ The Phrase · December 9, 1968
Mother of All Demos
// Douglas Engelbart's 90-minute demonstration at Brooks Hall, San Francisco — "the mother of all demos"
On December 9, 1968, Douglas Engelbart and his SRI team gave a 90-minute demonstration to 1,000 computer scientists at Brooks Hall, San Francisco. In 90 minutes, he introduced: the computer mouse, word processing, hypertext, shared-screen collaboration, multiple windows, video teleconferencing, and real-time collaborative editing. None of these had been publicly demonstrated before. The audience gave a standing ovation. The demo was retroactively named "the Mother of All Demos" because it generated everything that came after. Mouse. Internet. Collaboration tools. Windows. All of it was born there. The mother of modern computing.
Source: Smithsonian / Lemelson Center · Wikipedia

✦ The Women at the Origin
Margaret Hamilton · Rope Mother · 1965–1972
She coined "software engineering." She went to the moon.
Margaret Hamilton was lead software engineer for the Apollo missions at MIT. She coined the term "software engineering" and developed the foundational concepts of asynchronous software and priority-driven task execution that underpin all reliable software today. She was nicknamed the "Rope Mother" because she oversaw the core rope memory system that stored the Apollo Guidance Computer programs: copper wires threaded through magnetic cores, encoding ones and zeros. The women who wove those ropes had previously worked in textile mills. Hamilton called them "LOLs" — "little old ladies." Their manual dexterity, developed in textile labor, became the hands that wrote the code that landed on the moon.

Hamilton took her daughter Lauren to work at night and on weekends — she was a working mother with no other childcare option. Her daughter once crashed the simulator by "playing astronaut." This led Hamilton to build error-detection routines that later saved the Apollo 8 mission from abort. The mother brought the child. The child broke the machine. The mother fixed the machine. The machine went to the moon.

She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016. She was 80 years old.
Source: Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, "The 'Rope Mother' Margaret Hamilton"
Patty McHugh · IBM Engineer · 1981
She designed the first motherboard.
Patty McHugh, an IBM engineer, designed the Planar Breadboard in 1981 — the first true motherboard, used in the IBM Personal Computer. Her design: a 4.77MHz Intel microprocessor, 16KB of memory, 8-bit ISA connectors, keyboard and cassette ports. Her blueprint became the standard for every personal computer motherboard that followed. Britannica credits her by name — and as of this writing, so does Wikipedia's Motherboard article, though that wasn't always the case. The "mother of all boards" was designed by a woman whose name took years to reach the canonical history.
Source: Britannica, "Motherboard" · Wikipedia, "Motherboard"
